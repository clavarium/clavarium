Clavarium · app designs

Three first ideas for the installable app, each drawn for a different person. Same fixed structure: tools on top, the tool's own terminal in the middle, shortcuts, voice and keys at the bottom. Static mockups, nothing is wired.

The owner who uses it all daySpeed and density: the terminal gets every pixel, helpers under the thumb, dangerous keys out of reach.

A · Thumb-first terminal

One thin bar on top, three rows at the bottom, everything else is terminal. Safe things under the thumb, dangerous things far from it.

Why this design

  • The terminal owns the screen. One 36 px bar on top, three rows below; on a phone about two thirds of the height is raw terminal, and on a tablet the helpers move to a side rail instead of stacking.
  • One tap to approve. When the tool prints a numbered question, the options appear as big keys just above the helpers, with Yes on the thumb side — no keyboard, no aiming at a digit.
  • Thumb map. Enter, mic and shortcuts sit in the easy arc; Ctrl+C is in the far corner, dashed, and needs a hold; Revoke needs two taps and the confirm step puts Keep under the thumb.
  • Voice is a mode, not a dialog. The mic is the widest key of the bottom row and shows the live phrase in place; “auto” presses Enter for me, so a whole exchange can be hands-off.
  • Least sure: the approval bar depends on recognising prompts in raw terminal output across several tools. If detection is wrong even rarely, a big Yes key is the worst possible place for a mistake — it may need to stay a plain “1 / 2 / 3” row.

Why this design
Words first, key names second. Every key says what it does (Back, OK, Stop); the real key (Esc, Enter, Ctrl+C) is the small print.
Yes / No are the first shortcuts, always in the same place right under the terminal; each shows the key it sends, so nothing is parsed or hidden.
Voice is the biggest control, with "Send when I stop" on by default, so a whole exchange can happen without the keyboard.
The state line is a sentence ("Waiting for your answer"), and first launch is three numbered lines plus one button that also wakes the conversation.
Least sure: "Yes = 1, No = Esc" fits Claude Code but not every tool; the mapping may need to be set per tool, or the keys may mislead.

Why this design
  • Files are the result, so they sit next to the terminal: side panel on tablet, bottom sheet on phone, with the approval prompt still visible above.
  • "New since you last looked" is everywhere: a count on the Files button, a strip under the tools, a section at the top of the list, and one "Download 3 new" button.
  • Photo in two taps: the ＋ glued to Files, then Camera. The upload shows its progress and lands in in/.
  • File paths printed in the terminal are tappable and open the preview; the preview can type the path back into the terminal.
  • Least sure: the phone top is tall (tools + chips + new-files strip) and takes lines away from the terminal; the strip may need to fold into the Files badge.
